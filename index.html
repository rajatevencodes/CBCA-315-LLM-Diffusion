Base paper · What TAPE does

Let a big AI explain, then a small model learns from it

TAPE’s task: guess each paper’s topic (node classification) from its text and citations.

01 · ASK THE BIG AI

GPT-3.5 reads

The paper’s title and abstract, with the question: “Which topic is this? Rank your guesses and explain why.”

02 · ANSWER + REASON

Guess + why

A ranked list of likely topics and a short explanation, written as text.

03 · TEXT → NUMBERS

Small LM (DeBERTa)

Trained to turn text into features. Three sets: original text, explanation, guesses.

04 · ADD CITATIONS

Graph network (GNN)

Mixes each paper’s features with its neighbours’. One GNN per feature set; the three answers are averaged.

The idea in plain words: like a teacher who shows the working, not just the answer. The explanation carries extra knowledge that the small model learns from.

CBCA315 Minor Project · Topic 2

Can a Small AI Help
Find Research Topics?

Semantic Diffusion: budgeted LLM edge weights for community detection

Rajat Sharma (S24BCAU0134)  |  Guide: Dr. Soumita Das  |  Bennett University

Code and results: github.com/rajatevencodes/CBCA-315-LLM-Diffusion
The problem and the goal

From a big pile to papers on the right shelves

Today:

  • A huge pile of research papers.
  • We don’t know the topic of each paper.
  • Reading them all is too slow.

Desired state:

  • Every paper sits on the shelf of its own topic, with similar papers together.
So we ask a computer to do the sorting. But how can a computer sort papers it can’t read like we do? Next slide.
NOW: ONE BIG PILE, NO LABELS
GOAL: PAPERS ON SHELVES BY TOPIC
Each dot in the pile is a paper; each line is a citation.
How, and why only citations?

Citations are a free hint that two papers are related

Why citations?

  • Authors cite related work. A neural-network paper mostly cites other neural-network papers.
  • So each citation is a built-in hint that two papers are related.
  • It is already there: no reading, no labels, no AI needed.

How the computer uses it

  • Paper = dot. Citation = line.
  • Papers on the same topic cite each other a lot, so they clump together.
  • The computer finds the clumps. Each clump is a topic. This is community detection.
The catch: citations are only a hint, not a guarantee. Next slide.
Where it goes wrong

Not every citation means “same topic”

Both are citations, so a plain computer sees two identical lines.

The Adam line can pull the Transformer toward the wrong crowd and shelve it in the wrong place.

Our question: if we pay a small AI to read some of these pairs, do the shelves get better?
Source: Vaswani et al. (2017), arXiv:1706.03762, references [2] and [20]
Our aim

What are we trying to solve?

Aim: find out whether paying a small AI to re-check only the unsure citations gives better topic groups than free text matching, and whether any gain is worth the cost.

Five questions we answer

  1. Does text (W1) beat citations alone (W0)?
  2. Does the AI on about 10% of lines (W2) beat W1?
  3. Does choosing the unsure lines beat choosing random lines?
  1. How do quality and cost change with budget, prompt wording and model size?
  2. Do better groups pick better starting nodes for spreading information?

Dots, lines, and what “sure” means

Papers ≠ citations

Paper = a dot. Citation = a line between two dots. Real collections usually have more lines than dots.

Sure vs unsure

Each citation gets a text score from 0 to 1: how similar are the words of the two papers?

Like marks: 95 or 10 is a clear pass or fail. 45 is borderline, so look again.

“Sure” only means the score is clear, not that it is right. Real topics are never used here.

How the AI is used

The AI only sees the lines the computer is unsure about

Budget: at most 10% of all lines, the unsure ones nearest the middle (0.45). That is 528 lines in Cora and 4,432 in PubMed. The AI’s score replaces the W1 number on those lines.

So, was it successful?

A clear answer: not worth paying for

As an experiment: yes

  • Clear question, measured with real data.
  • Two real datasets, three detectors, 5 repeats.
  • Random-edge control and a significance test.
  • We know why it failed (score collapse).

As a fix: no

  • The small AI did not improve the groups.
  • Free text matching (W1) did as well as the paid AI (W2).
  • It cost money and the result got slightly worse.
Takeaway: a small AI cannot tell a close pair from a distant one here. A negative result still tells researchers where not to spend budget.
Source: project paper (Overleaf) and outputs/tables/
Where this comes from

Inspired by the course guide and recent LLM papers

Inspiration

  • Course guide, Topic 2 (Section 9): weight edges by text; use a small LLM only on ambiguous edges.
  • TAPE (He et al., ICLR 2024): source of Cora and PubMed with the original text.
  • ComGPT, CommLLM: LLMs for community detection, using large models.
  • LLM-GNN (Chen et al., ICLR 2024): spends an LLM budget on a few items, for a different task.

The gap we fill

  • Standard detectors treat every citation as equally important.
  • Big-LLM papers rarely report cost. Is an LLM better than cheap text matching? Unclear.
  • Using an LLM only on uncertain edges, under a fixed budget, is little explored.
We do not claim LLM edge weighting is new. Our contribution is a measured quality-vs-cost answer.
